# Notes, "On the Effects of Active Learning Environments in Computing Education"

## Overview

- studied effects of active learning classroom in an intro to programming course
- prior studies focused on the sciences and "were conducted by the same groups of researchers at three institutions" (267)
- specifically, this study aimed at replicating another study that found active learning environments to improve learning outcomes
- grouped students into three sections, comparing classroom design and pedagogical style

## Key Concepts

### Active learning classroom:

- Designed assuming that lecture halls are not best suited to active learning.
- "movable chairs, round tables, typically multimedia equipments to decentralize the role of the instructors and encourange peer communications" (267).
- expensive to build or to retrofit lecture halls ($100k to upgrade a 30-seat classroom)
- fits many fewer students

### Active learning

- This study focuses on peer instruction.
- Also "team-based learning, paired-programming"
- Other examples from a reference paper, Hossein Hakimzadeh, Raman Adaikkalavan, and Robert Batzinger. "Successful implementation of an active learning laboratory in computer science" (2011):
  - Group activities, like solve a problem, often on a whiteboard or with a computer display
  - Find minimum number of ten numbers, each of which is written on its own piece of paper. Students have another blank paper, which can store one number. Viewing one number at a time and the scratch pad, find the minimum number. Then return to solo seating and code it in a high-level language.

#### Peer Instruction

- students typically to reading and practice problems ahead of class
- students discuss the prep material in class and do more practice
- shown to improve "student achievement, satisfaction and self-efficacy" as well as to increase student retention in into programming courses (268)
- From Catherine H Crouch and Eric Mazur. Peer instruction: Ten years of experience
  and results. American journal of physics, 69(9):970–977, 2001:
  - "Peer Instruction engages students during class through activities that require each student to apply the core concepts being presented, and then to explain those concepts to their fellow students"
  - Students must to the relevant reading before class (this is presented as some kind of novelty, which surprised me).
  - Class is divided into several chunks, each of which goes through the following sequence:
    1. Instructor gives a short presentation, concluding with a conceptual question
    2. Students take a minute or two alone to formulate an answer.
    3. Students form groups and try to convince their peers of their answer.

##### Potential issue(s) with how the authors present peer instruction

They make it seem like the instructor no longer instructs:
"Peer instruction works more effective[ly] than conventional lecturing because instructor explanation is replaced and augmented with carefully designed questions that students committed [sic] time to work on" (268).

However, from a referenced paper covering 10 years of PI at MIT (1991 to 2001): "PI supports a student-centered learning environment by replacing some of lecture time’s traditional 'sage on the stage' activity with 'guide on the side' student-focused activity" ("Halving Fail Rates," 177).
Clicker questions (i.e. the polls we take today in our classes): first individually, then discuss with a couple peers, then retake, finally the instructor has some students explain their reasoning and goes over the correct answer.

## Replication Motivation

Two limits to prior studies:

1. "lack of control of confounding factors," namely the "influence of learning pedagogies" (268)
2. "lack of control of false positives" b/c "significance levels were not properly corrected" when comparing "multiple factors between two groups" (268)

## Study Design

Comparison between three courses:

1. conventional = conventional classroom + lecturing
2. active = active learning classroom + active learning and teaching
3. hybrid = active learing and teaching in a conventional classroom

- Same instructor for all three.
- Collected data on major, gender, and age.
- Same two midterms and final exam.

### Active Learning and Teaching

"students were assigned daily reading as well as a small quiz based on the textbook and videos that the instructor created; both of these were to be completed before the start of class. In addition, in Course Two and Three, students worked in groups of three to solve problems from material that they were required to read. The instructor as well as teaching assistants were available during this time to answer questions on the material" (269).

## Results and Dicussion

### Weird, possibly contradictory phrasing re age and gender

"a significant difference in terms of gender [...] was found.
Students in Course Three appeared to be older than their counterparts [...].
Although each section of the course was majority male (greater than 56% in all sections), it is worth noting that the percentage of female students is not significantly lower than male students" (269).

The ordering of information here is strange.
First they mention gender as significant, then the next thing they say has to do with age, after which they provide the "majority male" percentage, and conclude by saying that it's nos significant.

### ALE "insignificant"

The authors claim to have "successfully separated the effects of learning environments from other potentially confounding factors, among which the pedagogical approach is the most important one" (270).

A classroom designed to facilitate active learning, esp. group work, is insufficient for effective active learning experiences.

### Instruction technique is more important

It is more important that faculty are trained both in the teaching techniques and in how to use such a classroom.

"The findings of this study suggest that active learning and teaching can be conducted in conventional classrooms as effectively as in active learning classrooms" (271).

### Limitations

One institution, one instructor, one course, "only one active learning approach, peer instruction, was practiced in this experiment" (271).
That last aspect is probably the most glaring limitation.
Other active learning techniques may be more limited in their efficacy by convention classrooms.
